The six count columns represent two donors, each with one sample in the Rem, SARS and mock conditions at 5 days after infection. The count matrix contained 60,683 genes. Removing 27,356 genes with zero counts in all six samples left 33,327 genes before tool-specific filtering. This follows the gene-level filtering step discussed in Lecture 4: genes without counts cannot provide evidence of an expression change.

Both tools used the design ~ donor + condition. This accounts for differences between donors while estimating the condition effect. The pairing of the samples supports this design, and the PCA provides a visual check of donor-related variation (Figure 2.1).

PC1 explains 53% of the variation and separates the two donor groups. Donor 2 samples cluster together, while donor 3 samples are more spread out. This supports including donor in the model. As discussed in Lecture 4, PCA helps identify sample structure; it does not prove which factor causes all variation. With only two donors, the expression results remain evidence for this experiment rather than a general estimate for all COVID-19 patients.

2.1 Tool Selection and Preprocessing

DESeq2 (Love, Huber, and Anders 2014) and edgeR (Robinson, McCarthy, and Smyth 2009) were selected because both model RNA-seq counts with a negative binomial distribution. This allows the variance to be larger than the mean, as often occurs in these data. Lecture 4 also presents these tools as suitable choices, while noting that no method performs best in every dataset. Their different normalisation, filtering and dispersion procedures allow us to check whether a result depends on one analysis method. Data preparation used tidyverse (Wickham et al. 2019).

Data preprocessing was tailored to the requirements of each tool:

  • For DESeq2: Counts were normalised with the median-of-ratios method. During results generation, independent filtering was used to exclude low-information genes from multiple-testing adjustment. Genes without an adjusted p-value were excluded from the significant list.

  • For edgeR: filterByExpr removed genes with insufficient expression for the design. Library sizes were then recalculated and TMM normalisation was applied. Both normalisation methods account for differences in sequencing depth and RNA composition; the models use counts rather than TPM or FPKM.

2.2 Quality Control and Individual Tool Results

The dispersion plots show how both tools estimate variation between samples (Figure 2.2 and Figure 2.3). Low-count genes show a wider spread of estimates, while the fitted trends decline as expression increases.

For DESeq2, the black points are gene-wise dispersion estimates, the red line is the fitted trend and the blue points are the final estimates after shrinkage. The edgeR plot shows the biological coefficient of variation, the square root of dispersion, with gene-specific points and common and trend estimates. Sharing information across genes helps estimate variation with few replicates. These plots are useful checks, but they do not prove that every gene fits the model or that all significant results are true positives.

Both models tested the SARS condition against mock while accounting for donor. DESeq2 used its default Wald test, and edgeR used a GLM likelihood ratio test. Positive log2 fold changes indicate higher expression in SARS; negative values indicate lower expression.

2.3 Consensus Strategy

The significant lists were joined by gene identifier, retaining only genes significant in both tools. This intersection gives priority to results supported by both methods. It is conservative: a real expression change may be excluded if only one tool detects it. The tools also use the same samples, so their agreement is a sensitivity check rather than independent experimental confirmation.

A second filter required the DESeq2 log2 fold change and edgeR logFC to have the same sign. This avoids selecting a gene when the tools disagree on whether expression increased or decreased. The consensus table (Table 2.1) reports gene identifiers, annotations, effect sizes and adjusted significance values, and provides the candidates for Task 2.

Show analysis code 
consensus_table <- sig_deseq |>
  select(
    gene_id,
    fold_change_deseq = log2FoldChange,
    adj_p_value_deseq = padj
  ) |>
  inner_join(
    sig_edger |>
      select(gene_id, fold_change_edger = logFC, adj_p_value_edger = FDR),
    by = "gene_id"
  ) |> 
  filter(sign(fold_change_deseq) == sign(fold_change_edger)) |> 
  left_join(
    raw_counts_data |> 
      select(1:6),
    by = join_by(gene_id)
  ) |> 
  mutate(across(where(is.numeric), ~ as.character(signif(.x, 3))))

The consensus contains six genes: OAS2, GPRASP1, AC026801.2, IFI27, SERPINB2 and CXCL11. Five are upregulated; SERPINB2 is downregulated. This corresponds to 6 of the 7 edgeR hits and 6 of the 68 DESeq2 hits. The final rule did not retain 62 DESeq2 hits and 1 edgeR hit.

OAS2, GPRASP1 and AC026801.2 have the largest positive log2 fold changes, close to 10 or higher. IFI27 and CXCL11 show smaller positive changes, while SERPINB2 has similar negative estimates in both tools (-1.69 and -1.67). The annotation identifies AC026801.2 as a long non-coding RNA and the other five as protein-coding genes. Thus, the consensus includes both increased and decreased expression and more than one gene type.

Large effect estimates need care. GPRASP1 and AC026801.2 have zero counts in both mock samples, and OAS2 has only 0 and 2. The reported fold changes are model estimates without additional fold-change shrinkage. Low baseline expression and the small number of donors limit how precisely these large changes can be interpreted.

OAS2 was selected for Task 2 because both methods support increased expression and its role in the antiviral OAS pathway is described in the available literature (Merold et al. 2025). The largest fold change alone would not be enough to justify this choice. These results identify candidates for the project; they do not show that reducing any of these genes would improve recovery.
